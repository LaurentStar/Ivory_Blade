# Captain Ys

> *He looks like a man the way a shadow looks like the thing casting it.*

---

## Overview

| Field | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Name** | Captain Ys |
| **Species** | Unknown. Not human. Ghost-like entity with shape-shifting capabilities. |
| **Appearance** | Humanoid form. Pale white skin. White eyes with no pupils. Unsettling at a glance, wrong the longer you look. |
| **Role** | Captain of a small crew. Leader of the journey toward the center of the Mirralata System. |
| **Ship** | Commands a flying spaceship used for inter-realm travel. |
| **Motivation** | Believes the protagonist is a prophecy child. Offered to escort her on that basis. His deeper agenda is unknown. |

---

## Nature

Ys is not human. He resembles one in basic shape -- two arms, two legs, a head, a torso -- but the resemblance is surface-level and fails under scrutiny.

His skin is pale white. Not pink-white, not sun-deprived white. White like something that was never alive in the way flesh is alive. His eyes are the same: white, unbroken, no pupils. Looking at him triggers an instinctive wrongness. He does not blink at the right intervals. His expressions are close enough to human to be readable but far enough to be uncomfortable.

He is a shape-shifting ghost-like entity. He can alter his form -- the extent and limits of this ability are unclear. There is something incorporeal about him. Whether he is a spirit, an entity from outside the normal realm system, something that was once alive and is no longer, or something else entirely remains unknown.

**Nobody knows what he is.** Not the crew. Not the mercenary who has known him for years. Not even the rabbit girl -- his closest bond, the woman who is essentially his wife. She has traveled with him, fought beside him, shared whatever private moments they share, and she still does not know what Captain Ys actually is.

He does not refuse to explain. That is the joke.

Every time Ys begins to explain what he is -- every time the moment arrives where he opens his mouth and the answer is about to come out -- something happens. An attack. An explosion. An alarm. A crisis that demands immediate attention. The explanation is abandoned, the crew shifts to survival mode, and by the time the danger passes, everyone has forgotten to ask again. The next time someone remembers to bring it up, the cycle repeats.

This is a running bit. It happens enough times that it stops being coincidence and starts being comedy. The crew has collectively failed to learn what their captain is because the universe will not stop trying to kill them long enough for him to finish a sentence about it. Whether Ys is aware of the pattern, whether he finds it funny, or whether he is somehow causing it is itself an open question that will never be answered because something will interrupt before anyone can ask.

## Personality

Ys is **mysterious by nature, not by choice.** He does not perform secrecy. He simply does not volunteer information. Ask him a direct question and he may answer, but the answer will raise three more questions. Ask him what he is and he will look at you with those white eyes and say something that technically responds to the question without actually telling you anything.

He is **calm.** Not performatively calm, not suppressing something underneath. Calm in the way that suggests whatever is happening does not surprise him or was already accounted for. This is either deeply reassuring or deeply unsettling depending on the situation.

He is **decisive.** When he gives an order, it is not open for discussion. The crew follows because he hired them and because he has proven -- through means the protagonist is not yet privy to -- that his decisions keep people alive.

He is **stern.** He gives orders and expects them followed. He does not soften commands with pleasantries or cushion bad news. This is how he operates and the crew has learned to accept it.

He is **selectively humorous.** Ys is not above jokes -- but he only cracks them with the crew members he is least close to. The mechanic and the mouse woman get the dry remarks, the offhand comments that break tension. The rabbit girl and the mercenary almost never get this side of him. With them, he is serious. The closer someone is to Ys, the less he performs for them. The humor is a social tool for the outer circle, not a window into warmth.

He is **protective in practice, not in words.** He does not announce that he cares about his crew's survival. He simply makes decisions that keep them alive, positions himself between danger and the non-combatants, and adjusts plans when someone is at risk. The mechanic in particular -- she is not a fighter, and Ys goes out of his way to keep her out of conflicts. He does not explain why. He just does it.

The care is always subtle. A route changed to avoid a threat nobody else noticed yet. A supply stop that happens to stock exactly what someone needed but did not ask for. A decision that looks pragmatic on the surface but, if you trace it back, was made because one person on the crew would have been in danger otherwise. Ys does not draw attention to these things. He does not wait for a thank you. Most of the time, the crew does not even realize he did anything -- the danger they were steered away from is invisible precisely because he steered them away from it.

People do not appreciate what they do not see. Ys knows this. It is enough for him that they are alive and unaware rather than grateful and dead. The care is in the outcome, not in the recognition. If the crew never realizes what he does for them, that is fine. If they do notice and never say anything, that is also fine. He is not doing it for them to notice. He is doing it because that is what he does.

## The Prophecy

Ys believes the protagonist is a prophecy child. This is why he offered to escort her. This is the foundation of his involvement in her journey.

What the prophecy says, where it comes from, and whether it is real are open questions. He has not explained the prophecy to the protagonist in detail. She does not believe she is anyone's prophecy child. She accepted the escort because she needs passage, not because she shares his conviction.

Both armies in the Swahili Realm also believe the protagonist is the prophecy child -- a figure who can end their war in decisive victory for whichever side claims her. Whether their belief comes from the same prophecy Ys references, a different one, or a corrupted version of the same source is unknown.

**What Ys knows and the protagonist does not:** the prophecy ends with the protagonist as a sacrifice. At the end of the journey, she is not meant to survive. She is meant to be consumed by whatever process restores, resolves, or fulfills the prophecy's promise. Ys knows this. He has known it from the beginning. He escorted her knowing he is walking her toward her death.

This is the hidden weight behind everything Ys does with the protagonist.

**Ys and the earring:** Ys has examined the vessel containing Kim Tiernan -- a thumb-sized container the protagonist wears as an earring. He knows what Kim is, what state he is in, and how the containment works. More importantly, he knows that the earring is the only reason the protagonist is on this journey. If the earring is lost -- if Kim is obliterated -- she will not willingly continue toward the center of the Mirralata System. Her motivation evaporates entirely. No Kim, no prophecy child walking toward her role. Ys understands this completely. The earring is not just the protagonist's emotional anchor. It is the thing that keeps his mission viable. Protecting the earring is not an act of kindness. It is operational necessity.

### Ys and the Protagonist

Their relationship is complex. It does not fit the pattern he has with anyone else on the crew.

He is not close to her -- not the way he is close to the rabbit girl or the mercenary. She is not his partner, not his mentee, not someone he has history with. She is the prophecy child. She is the mission.

But he also does not joke with her the way he jokes with the mechanic or the mouse woman. The dry humor, the offhand remarks he uses to manage the outer circle -- those are absent with the protagonist. With her, he is **monotone.** Flat. Direct. Orders given without inflection. Questions answered without warmth or sharpness. He is not stern the way he is with the mercenary (stern because he cares), and he is not light the way he is with the mechanic (light because she is not close enough to warrant seriousness). He is simply... level. Neutral in a way that reads as disinterest but is actually something worse.

He is being **more ruthless with her** than with anyone else on the crew. The decisions he makes regarding the protagonist are harder, colder, more calculated. He puts her in situations the others are shielded from. He does not go out of his way to protect her the way he protects the mechanic. He does not mentor her the way he mentors the mercenary. He lets the danger reach her because she needs to be strong enough for what comes at the end -- and because, on some level, investing emotionally in someone you are delivering to their death is a line even Ys will not cross.

The monotone is a wall. If he jokes with her, he starts to like her. If he mentors her, he starts to care. If he cares, he cannot do what the prophecy requires. So he keeps it flat. Professional. Distant in a way that is different from how he is distant with everyone else -- because with everyone else, the distance is personality. With the protagonist, the distance is survival. His survival, emotionally, not hers.

The crew does not see this clearly. They see Ys being Ys -- stern, quiet, unreadable. The protagonist may sense that he treats her differently but cannot pin down why.

The rabbit girl knows. She is the only one who knows.

She knows the prophecy ends in sacrifice. She knows what Ys is escorting the protagonist toward. She knows why he keeps his voice flat and his distance measured. She carries the same knowledge he does -- and she handles it completely differently.

The rabbit girl is **bubbly and fun** with the protagonist. Warm. Present. She does not build a wall. She does not go monotone. She treats the protagonist like a person, not a delivery. Whether this is because she processes the knowledge differently than Ys, because she believes something can change, or because she has decided that the girl deserves kindness on the walk to the altar -- that is her own quiet burden. She does not explain it to Ys, and he does not ask her to stop.

The contrast between Ys's flatness and the rabbit girl's warmth toward the protagonist is one of the most telling dynamics on the ship. Two people who share the same terrible knowledge. One who shuts down to survive it. One who opens up despite it.

## The Crew

Ys did not hire everyone on the crew. Some he chose. Some came to him. The result is a small group with uneven ties to him -- two people who know him deeply, two who barely know him at all, and a protagonist who is there for her own reasons entirely.

### Kat Arlean Mori -- The Hidden Partner

Kat is Ys's partner. Not a colleague, not a close friend -- his partner in the way that matters most. Something like a wife.

Nobody on the crew knows this. Ys and Kat do not act affectionate toward each other in front of the others. There is no visible warmth between them that would distinguish their relationship from any other captain-crew dynamic. She follows his orders like everyone else. He treats her with the same stern tone he uses on the mercenary. To the rest of the crew, she is another hire -- the alien girl with rabbit features who happens to be one of two people Ys seems to trust slightly more than the others.

The concealment is not dramatic. They are not hiding a scandal. They simply do not perform their relationship for an audience. Whatever exists between them exists in private, in glances that mean something only to them, in decisions Ys makes that happen to keep her safe without being traceable to favoritism.

This is the secret at the core of Ys's character: the ghost-like entity who seems to exist without attachment has the deepest attachment on the ship. He is not cold. He is private.

### Brod Tsumi -- The Mentee

Brod has known Ys for a while. Their relationship predates this crew, this ship, and this mission. Ys has been pulling the boy into dangerous situations for long enough that the pattern is familiar to both of them.

From Brod's perspective, this journey is Ys getting him into yet another bout of craziness. It is not the first time, and he suspects it will not be the last. He follows because the previous bouts -- however insane -- made him better. He learned. He grew. He became more capable in ways that a normal life would not have provided. Ys knows this. It is exactly to Ys's liking.

Ys takes on a mentorship role with Brod. Not a gentle one. He is stern, gives orders, and expects them executed. The mentorship is in the doing, not in the explaining. He puts Brod in situations that force growth and trusts the boy to figure it out. When Brod does figure it out, Ys does not praise him. He gives him the next problem.

Ys does not joke with Brod. The boy is too close for that. The humor Ys occasionally shows is reserved for the outer circle -- the mechanic, the mouse woman. With Brod, everything is direct and serious. This is how the boy knows Ys respects him: the ghost doesn't waste his time pretending things are lighter than they are.

### The Mechanic -- The Passenger He Protects

The mechanic was not recruited. She was picked up. The crew encountered her while she was traveling on public transport. When chaos unfolded and public routes became unsafe, it became much safer to use their own ship. Since she was heading the same direction as the crew, Ys decided to bring her along.

She is useful. Ys normally handles the ship's mechanical needs himself, but she is a good substitute for these tasks. She keeps the ship and equipment running, freeing Ys to focus on navigation and command.

She is not a fighter. Despite her tall, lanky frame, she has no combat training and no instinct for violence. This makes her the most vulnerable member of the crew in every conflict situation. Ys goes out of his way to make sure she does not get killed -- positioning her behind cover, adjusting plans to avoid putting her in crossfires, making calls that prioritize her survival alongside the mission. He does not explain this protectiveness. She is on his ship. That is enough.

### The Mouse Woman -- The Stray with a Mission

Vennessa was not part of the original crew. A wealthy collector took her star cat and retreated to the Mayler Realm. The galaxy-sized worm guardian blocked cross-realm travel for regular people -- but not the rich. Vennessa was stranded with no way to follow.

She never asked Ys. She knew what the answer would be. She **stowed away** -- hid on the ship until it was too late to turn back. By the time Ys found her, turning back was not viable. He accepted it with cold pragmatism: she is here, she works.

She can perform drawing-casting magic -- the same general type the mercenary uses, at a similar skill level. The difference is tools: the mercenary's dual swords function as ink pens (fine lines, functional results), while Vennessa's pen brush is a broad brush (sweeping strokes, detailed creations). Her output looks more refined because her instrument allows it, not because she is more talented.

She is small and not built for combat. Her magic is her only tool in a fight, and even then she is better suited to support and utility than direct confrontation. She is not a soldier. She is a small woman looking for her cat who snuck onto a ship full of people being hunted across an active warzone.

Ys really wants her gone. But the Mayler Realm -- the only destination that makes sense for Vennessa -- is **close to the center of the Mirralata System**. There is no convenient stop along the way. Dropping her anywhere else means stranding her in a realm that is not her destination with no way to reach her cat. She is going to be on his ship for **nearly the entire journey**. He did not invite her. She did not ask. And he is stuck with her for almost every game.

### Crew Summary

| Member | How They Joined | Relationship to Ys | Combat Role |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Kat Arlean Mori** | Pre-existing. His partner. | Hidden wife. Closest bond on the ship. | Unknown |
| **Brod Tsumi** | Pre-existing. Long history together. | Mentee. Stern, serious, no jokes. | Combatant. Drawing-casting magic. |

Everyone on the crew believes the same baseline truth: they are hired to get from point A to point B by a strange ghost man. The rabbit girl's true relationship with Ys, the depth of his mentorship with the mercenary, and his quiet calculations about who stays and who gets dropped off -- none of this is spoken aloud. The crew operates on orders and proximity, not intimacy.

## Combat

Ys fights. The ghost can kill.

### The Staff

His primary weapon is a **pike/staff** -- a pole weapon he uses to poke, thrust, and keep distance. It is not a simple stick. The weapon has multiple configurations:

**Staff mode (default):** A pole weapon. He jabs, sweeps, and controls space with it. Straightforward and efficient.

**Scythe mode:** The staff transforms into a scythe. The reach stays, but the cutting geometry changes -- hooking, pulling, and cleaving instead of thrusting. This is for killing, not controlling.

**Laser mode:** The weapon can fire a **singular powerful laser beam** -- a sharp, focused cut that bisects a single target cleanly. One shot, one line, one thing split in half. This is not a rapid-fire weapon or an area-of-effect tool. It is a precision execution.

**Buzz-saw mode:** Green fins protrude from the pole, fanning out like holding a green umbrella. The fins spin at high speed, creating a **rotating cutting disc** around the weapon. In this configuration, the staff functions as both offense and defense -- it shreds anything that comes close and blocks incoming projectiles by chewing them apart. The visual is distinctive: a pale ghost man holding a spinning green buzz saw on a stick.

### Hand-to-Hand

Ys is the **second-best hand-to-hand fighter on the crew.** He is not the protagonist's equal in close-quarters unarmed combat, but he is competent enough that most opponents would not survive the mistake of getting inside his staff range and assuming they had the advantage.

### Ranged -- Guns vs. Piloting

His aim with firearms is **bad.** Put a gun in his hands and he will miss shots that the mercenary would land without thinking. This is a genuine weakness, not a stylistic choice. He does not carry guns because he cannot use them effectively.

His aim while **piloting a flying craft is the opposite** -- extremely sharp. Whatever disconnect exists between Ys and handheld firearms disappears when he is at the controls of a ship. He can thread a craft through tight spaces and line up strafing runs with precision that borders on mechanical. The contrast is stark: hand him a pistol and he is mediocre, put him in a cockpit and he is lethal.

## Role in This Game

During the events of the first game (the Swahili Realm), Ys is the crew's leader and navigator. He is the reason they are on this journey and the reason the protagonist has passage at all.

When the crew is captured by the wolf squad, Ys is among the captured. The protagonist rescues them all. This is notable: the mysterious, seemingly all-knowing captain was taken. He is not invincible. He is not omniscient. Whatever his nature is, it has limits.

After the rescue, the crew's dynamic shifts. The protagonist has proven she is more than cargo. Whether Ys acknowledges this shift openly, adjusts his behavior, or continues as before is a question of character.

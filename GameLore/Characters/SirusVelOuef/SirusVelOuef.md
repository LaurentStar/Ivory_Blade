# Sirus Vel Ouef

> *He did not want the baby. He argued to leave her behind. Then he spent the next seventeen years proving he was wrong.*

---

## Before the Protagonist

Sirus was **eighteen years old** when he and Sorain came across a human baby in the aftermath of a war that had destroyed the protagonist's birth country. Eighteen -- barely an adult even by human standards, and for an elf who will live a thousand years, practically a child himself. He and his sister were young elves navigating a warzone, not seasoned adults making measured decisions.

Sorain wanted to take the baby. Sirus argued against it. A human infant in a warzone was not their responsibility, and bringing a human child into elven society would invite complications -- social, practical, biological. Elves live a thousand years. This child would be dead before Sirus reached middle age.

He was overruled. Sorain picked up the baby, and they brought her along when they relocated to an elf-controlled nation using their family connections.

---

## The Early Years

Sirus was not cold to the protagonist as a child. He was not absent or neglectful. He made the protagonist feel loved from the beginning. He was present, steady, and warm in the quiet way that defined everything he would later become to her. He simply did not yet see himself as her teacher or her guide in any formal sense. He was a young elf who had a human child in his household and who treated her with genuine care.

He watched Sorain's compassion slowly curdle. He saw his sister rescue a baby out of genuine feeling and then, over time, come to see that child as temporary -- a brief distraction in a thousand-year life. When Sorain had her own biological child, a full-blooded elf, the shift became undeniable. The protagonist faded into the background of Sorain's attention.

Sirus saw what this did to the protagonist. The confusion. The loneliness. The slow, quiet understanding that the person who had saved her life did not consider her a real daughter. He felt it. He did not confront the sister directly or intervene in dramatic fashion. What he did was quieter: he stayed. He was there. He made sure the protagonist knew that someone in her life saw her as real and permanent, even if the sister did not.

---

## The Shift -- When He Started Training Her

Sirus did not begin training the protagonist in magic from the start. That came later, and it came because of her.

The protagonist was eight years old when she encountered Elidyr T. Lumo, a Spellblade Captain passing through town on a three-month stay. Elidyr scolded her off the streets and told her to get smarter and get stronger. The protagonist took it as a direct order and spent the next three months reporting to the area near Elidyr's 3rd house for daily instruction. When Elidyr left, the protagonist kept following those orders on her own.

Sirus noticed the change. The protagonist had come back from those three months with something new -- a discipline, a focus, a visible interest in magic and self-improvement that had not been there before. She was not just existing in the elven world anymore. She wanted to learn. She wanted to be capable.

That was when Sirus stepped in.

He had always made her feel loved. Now he started teaching her. He began training her in magic, and he held her to the same standards he would hold any elf child -- not because he was harsh, but because lowering the bar would mean agreeing with everyone who believed a human could not keep up. He refused to be one of those people.

This was the practical expression of who Sirus had become to the protagonist: the person who treated her as capable and then demanded she live up to it.

---

## As a Teacher

Sirus eventually got the protagonist enrolled in school alongside elf children. She entered far behind -- like an eight-year-old starting formal education for the first time while her classmates had years of foundation.

Elves pick up magic better than humans. This is biology, not effort or character. The protagonist was always lagging behind her elf classmates no matter how hard she worked. She could learn what they learned, but never at their pace.

Sirus did not adjust his expectations downward. He did not tell her it was okay to be slower, or that she should be proud of doing well "for a human." He held the same line he held for any student: this is the standard, meet it. If it takes longer, it takes longer. The bar does not move.

This approach meant the protagonist was always struggling, always behind, always grinding. It also meant she was always improving. She never caught up to her elf peers in raw ability, but she became competent -- functional in magic in a way that no one expected from a human child.

---

## Love Life -- The Older Woman

Sirus's love life is not a clean story.

When asked about his child's mother, Sirus says his **girlfriend died in the war** -- the same war that orphaned the protagonist. It is a clean, sympathetic story. A young elf who lost his love in the same conflict that brought him an adopted daughter. Nobody questions it. Nobody digs.

**It is a lie.**

After settling in the elf-controlled nation, Sirus caught the attention of an **older elf woman** -- a married woman with influence and connections. She provided **favors** to the siblings: employment, opportunity, access to the social infrastructure that allowed them to build a life in elven society. The druid position maintaining the estate garden, the ability to get the protagonist into school -- much of what Sirus has comes through her.

She was **tired of her old husband** -- a man who was never around and never emotionally present. She picked up this young elf man and began a **secret affair**. She chose him because he was young, present, and willing to be what her husband was not.

The affair **accidentally resulted in a pregnancy**. She decided to have the child live with Sirus rather than risk exposing the affair to her husband. Both Sirus and his biological child are **under her thumb** -- she controls the favors, the employment, the opportunities. The arrangement is transactional. Sirus gets stability for his family. She gets a lover and a hidden child. Neither can walk away cleanly.

Sirus currently has a **girlfriend** -- a real relationship, separate from the older woman. But he still **reports to the older elf woman**. The affair is not over in any practical sense. She still holds leverage. He still takes what she provides.

This is the part of Sirus that is less than honorable. The steady, loving father who never wavered in his devotion to the protagonist is also a man who sleeps with a married woman for access, keeps a secret child arrangement, and maintains a real girlfriend while none of the threads are clean. He is willing to be **less honorable** when it serves his family's survival -- and this willingness, this pragmatic flexibility with morality, **chipped off into the protagonist** in unrelated ways. She did not learn it from him directly. She absorbed it by proximity -- the understanding that good people sometimes do compromised things, and that the world does not always reward clean hands.

---

## His Own Child

Sirus's biological child was born when the protagonist was **five years old** -- the product of the affair with the older elf woman. By elven standards, this is extraordinarily early -- an elf having a child at roughly 23 is almost unheard of in a species that lives a millennium. But raising a human baby changed something in both siblings. Watching a child grow at human speed -- fast, urgent, every year visibly different -- made Sirus and Sorain want their own families sooner than any elf would normally consider. They both found partners and had children early, far ahead of the elven timeline.

Sorain's early parenthood deepened the problem. Having her own biological elf child only sharpened the contrast with the human daughter she had rescued -- a child she increasingly saw as temporary. For Sirus, the opposite happened.

This changed nothing about his relationship with the protagonist.

Where Sorain's love fractured along the line of biology -- shifting entirely to her biological child once it was born -- Sirus's held. He treated both children equally. His blood and his choice carried the same weight. He did not favor one over the other, did not compare them, did not treat the protagonist as lesser now that he had a child of his own species and his own blood.

This is perhaps the most important thing Sirus ever did for the protagonist. Not the magic training, not the school enrollment, not the daily steadiness. The moment he had every biological and social reason to prioritize his own child and chose not to -- that was the proof that his love for the protagonist was not charity, not pity, not a phase. It was permanent.

## Who He Is

Sirus is not loud. He is not dramatic. He does not make speeches about love or belonging or what family means. He is the kind of person whose presence is so steady that you do not notice it until it is gone, and then you realize it was holding everything together.

He shows love through investment. Teaching the protagonist magic was not separate from loving her -- it was the same act. Getting her into school was love. Holding her to standards was love. Refusing to lower the bar was love. He does not distinguish between caring for someone and pushing them to be better, because in his view they are the same thing.

He is patient in the way that elves can afford to be -- measured in centuries, not years. But his patience with the protagonist is different from elven patience. He is not waiting for her to grow up in the way an elf waits for a century to pass. He is paying attention to every year, every month, every small improvement. He is present on the human timescale, not the elven one. He chose to experience her childhood at her speed, not his.

He did not confront Sorain about her treatment of the protagonist. This is not cowardice. It is a recognition that he could not change his sister's nature -- he could only make sure the protagonist had someone who saw her differently. He chose to be the counterweight rather than fight a battle he could not win.

## Relationship with the Protagonist

### What He Gave Her
- **Emotional foundation.** Before the magic, before the training, before Elidyr or the Black Rose -- Sirus made her feel loved. That came first and never wavered.
- **Elven standards without elven condescension.** He demanded she meet the same bar as any elf child, which was the most respectful thing anyone in her life ever did for her.
- **Proof that love is not conditional on biology.** When he had his own child and nothing changed, the protagonist learned that family is a choice, not an inheritance.

### What He Could Not Give Her
- **Protection from Sorain.** He could not make his sister love the protagonist the way he did. He could only be present as the alternative.
- **Enough.** His training made her competent for a human. It did not make her capable of surviving what she would eventually face. That required the Black Rose and the Blaze Network -- things Sirus knows nothing about.
- **An answer to what she is.** Sirus could love her, train her, and raise her. He could not tell her who she is, because she is something no one in the elven world has a framework for: a human who is culturally elven, magically trained, and biologically out of place.
